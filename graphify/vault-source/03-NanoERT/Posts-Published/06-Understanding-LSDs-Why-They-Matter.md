# Understanding LSDs Why They Matter

Understanding Lysosomal Storage Diseases (LSDs): Why They Matter More Than Ever.

LSDs are a group of rare, inherited, metabolic disorders. In LSDs, the cell's recycling centers — the lysosomes — fail to break down molecules, leading to their toxic accumulation. LSDs are individually rare (50-70 different LSDs), yet collectively affect ~1 in 5,000-7,500 births.

What happens in LSDs: lysosomes use enzymes to break down carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and cellular waste. When lysosomal dysfunction occurs, substrates accumulate, cells become overwhelmed, causing toxicity, inflammation, and progressive tissue/organ damage — affecting the brain and nervous system, liver and spleen, heart, skeletal system, eyes, skin, kidneys and pancreas.

Types of LSDs: lipidoses (Wolman disease, cholesteryl ester storage disease), mucopolysaccharidoses/MPS (Hurler syndrome, Hunter syndrome), sphingolipidoses (Gaucher disease, Fabry disease, Niemann-Pick, Krabbe, Tay-Sachs, Sandhoff), and others (Batten disease, cystinosis, Danon disease, Pompe disease).

No cure is currently available, only symptomatic treatments, and there are no non-invasive, effective treatments reaching the brain. Advances in ERT, substrate reduction therapy (SRT), stem cell transplantation (HSCT), and emerging gene therapies are transforming lives.
