# Were We Wrong With Parkinsons Disease

Were we wrong with Parkinson's disease from the very start? Parkinson's disease has long been understood through the lens of dopaminergic neuron loss and alpha-synuclein aggregation. Were we wrong? No. PD diagnosis has long been established through the appearance of, especially but not exclusively, motor symptoms. Were we wrong? No.

We were simply looking through a telescope when we needed a microscope. Now we know that PD is complex. Yet lysosomal dysfunction is rising as a key agent in its pathogenesis. The lysosomal system acts as a recycling and detoxifying hub. When this network goes awry, cells accumulate toxic compounds, leading to their death.

Our approach: research is continuously showing that we can restore lysosomal function and improve disease pathogenesis by delivering active lysosomal enzymes to cells. Priming stability and therapeutic efficacy, our first asset surrounds GBA.

GBA1 mutations cause lysosomal dysfunction and promote accumulation of misfolded alpha-synuclein in dopaminergic neurons — increasingly viewed as causal, not merely associative. GCase deficiency leads to impaired clearance of alpha-synuclein, dysregulated lipid metabolism and lysosomal biogenesis, heightened oxidative and ER stress, and disrupted mitophagy.

GBA in numbers: 1st PD risk factor; ~10-12% of idiopathic PD patients carry GBA1 variants (~25% of Ashkenazi Jewish PD populations); faster disease progression, earlier onset, and cognitive decline. Emerging models suggest other PD risk genes (LRRK2, VPS35, ATP13A2, CTSD) converge on endolysosomal pathways.

If Parkinson's is, at its core, a lysosomal disorder — should we shift how we stratify, diagnose, and treat it?
